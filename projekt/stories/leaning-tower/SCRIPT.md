## Facts and sources
| # | Fact used in the script | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundations laid 9 August 1173. | Wikipedia, "Leaning Tower of Pisa" |
| 2 | It began to sink in 1178, after construction reached the second floor. | Wikipedia |
| 3 | Weak, unstable subsoil; a foundation only 3 m deep. | Wikipedia |
| 4 | Construction halted for most of a century because Pisa was almost continually at war with Genoa, Lucca and Florence. The pause let the soil settle, which likely kept the tower from toppling. | Wikipedia |
| 5 | Work resumed in 1272; the upper floors were built with one side taller than the other, so the tower is curved. | Wikipedia |
| 6 | 1934: Mussolini had grout/concrete pumped into the foundation to straighten it; the tilt got worse. Amounts vary by source (80–200 t), so no number is used. | Mental Floss; PBS NOVA "Interventions"; HISTORY.com |
| 7 | The tilt reached 5.5° by 1990; it closed to the public on 7 January 1990. | Wikipedia |
| 8 | 1993–2001: soil removed from under the raised side straightened it by 45 cm (to its 1838 position). Reopened 15 December 2001 (11 years closed). | Wikipedia |
| 9 | WWII: US Army sergeant Leon Weckstein, sent to confirm whether Germans were using it as an observation post, was struck by its beauty and did not call in artillery. | Wikipedia (from Weckstein's own account) |

## Narration (paste into ElevenLabs)
The Leaning Tower of Pisa is still standing because of a war.

In 1173, Pisa started building a grand bell tower. Five years later, after just two floors, it began to tip.

The ground underneath was soft clay and sand, and the foundation was only three metres deep.

Luckily for the tower, Pisa went to war.

Unluckily for Pisa, it kept going to war. For almost a hundred years.

Nobody touched the tower. And slowly, the soft ground squeezed together and got firm. Like memory foam: it sinks when you sit on it, then it holds you.

When the builders finally came back, they made the new floors taller on the leaning side. So the tower doesn't just lean. It's bent. Like a banana.

In 1934, Mussolini ordered it straightened. His engineers pumped concrete into the ground. It leaned even more.

By 1990, it was tilting five and a half degrees, so they closed it. Engineers spent eleven years gently pulling soil out from under the high side, and its top moved back forty-five centimetres.

But its luckiest day came earlier. In 1944, an American sergeant was sent to check if German soldiers were using it as a lookout. He decided it was too beautiful to destroy.

So war saved it. Twice.
